# Breachline lore codex

The lore codex is an in-game book of short entries about the world. This file is where the entries are written and reviewed. Once the creator approves an entry, it moves into the game.

## How it works (creator, 2026-11-13)

- **Where:** it lives in the Collection screen, which is part of the creator's UI refresh.
- **Unlocking:** entries might unlock as you play, through chapters cleared and cards owned. This isn't decided yet.
- **Length:** one to three short paragraphs per entry.
- **Related cards:** entries don't list them. Listing them stays an option for later.
- **Art:** any art already used is fair game, especially concept art and card art. Card art is fine even when it was made from an image in `art/nsfw/`, because the card's copy lives elsewhere. Never take an image directly from `art/nsfw/`.
- **Content:** settled lore is the base. Small invented details are welcome when they fit and break no rule: names, places, towns, bands, devices, customs and sayings. They're listed at the end of each batch for verdicts.
- **Process:** entries are written here first for thumbs, and then the screen is built.

## The voice (creator, 2026-11-13)

**The writer** is a Gnosis scholar in Ambrel, cataloguing the world as a historian would. They are fair-minded but have a Gnosis view: a little proud of their own side, wry about the others, and frightened of the Dominion. They quote the people they've met, with a short credit such as "a priest of the Abbey of the Long Stair".

**They talk about the world as they know it.** Think of how someone describes their country's political parties or a neighbouring nation: who those people are, where they live, what they're like and what others think of them. It isn't a list of policies or a restatement of the setting's premise.
- Keep caveats light. Too many "though not always" lines read like a rules document.
- Keep descriptions of looks short. The art shows what they look like, and the writer mentions only what stands out.
- Leave background in the background. An entry doesn't need to say everything that's true.

**They take everyday things for granted.** A Roman historian recorded battles and who led them, but never how hand-to-hand fighting worked, because everyone knew. A modern story says someone flicked on a light without explaining electricity. So:
- Magic isn't explained.
- Healing and revival show up as assumptions, for example a healer saying the rest deserved their rest.
- Only what a person in the world would find remarkable gets explained, such as Ambrel's university, the goldmark or a people rarely met.

**What the reference books do** (studied 2026-11-13 from the Dragon Age codex, mainly Brother Genitivi and Sister Petrine, and *Pocket Guide to the Empire*):
- **Open with a confident thesis or a paradox,** such as "a land of shocking extremes" or "the poorest nation and the most feared".
- **Fill the entry with concrete, named specifics:** named places, gods, fortresses, rulers and dates. A custom is described exactly, such as a wedding where the knots untied while a hymn is sung set the years of the marriage, and the custom reveals the character of the people.
- **Use history only where it explains the present,** in one or two sentences.
- **Let the writer's bias show** through judgement, not commentary.
- **Keep humour dry,** from understatement and specific absurd detail ("three wars over elopements, five over wool, one over an apple tree"), never from a punchline.
- **Write measured, complete sentences in a slightly formal register.** No run of short punchy fragments.
- **Close on a pointed line** that draws the meaning together, such as "And yet the Imperium survives."
- **Use "I" rarely,** and for honest admission more than anecdote.
- **Keep entries to about 80–200 words.** Credits for other in-world authors and quoted people go at the foot.

**A glimpse of the world as it sees itself** (creator, 2026-11-13, third review). The codex is for players. It is not a catalogue of the lore, since `SETTING.md` already keeps that. Each entry should tell players something new rather than rehash the setting: a custom, a saying, a habit, a named place or a piece of history.
- Broad, confident generalisations are good, the way a well-read person describes a people they know. The liked entries (L11 Gnosis, L14 Genesis, L15 Sunmane Oath) work this way.
- The writer's own encounters are rare. Most entries have none, and a run of "I once met…" becomes a tic.
- No quips about setting facts. Humour comes from the writer's point of view, never from a clever turn of phrase.

**Show, don't tell** (creator, 2026-11-13, second review). A short scene, one detail or a quote does more than a summary.
- Say a thing once. "You might see one in Ambrel once a year" already says lion folk are rare, so don't add that the street stops to look.
- Use plain words with a good rhythm. Avoid odd, roundabout phrasing: "it collects its tithes in coin and souls", not "in coin and in things that aren't coin".
- Nothing on the nose. Quotes and lines should come at a thing sideways, not announce it.
- Don't rehash an earlier entry's joke or point.
- Keep a layer between the writer and the setting notes, the way the art keeps one between the painting and the card rules. The setting is design notes: "orcs are never Vigil" explains why the world is the way it is, and an in-world writer would never say it.

**The truth about the gods** can be hinted at, never stated. A summoner who feels something "very far away" holding the other end is the right size of hint.

**Don't harp on far-off places.** A rare mention of sailors' stories is enough.

**Never in the codex:**
- the Mountain's Lung
- game terms such as cards, mana, units or colours

## Batch 1: history, the gods, the alignments, the factions and the peoples (final, creator-approved 2026-11-13)

Written in the register of the reference books, and approved by the creator as final. The Dominion, Artificers, Ashen Court, Warhorde and humans entries are unchanged from the approved second draft. Art lines are suggestions; I'll confirm the files when the screen is built.

#### L0 · A note from the compiler
*Art: none*

This book is an attempt to set down the world as it stands, a hundred and fifty years after the Cataclysm, for readers who have not had the leisure to walk it. It is not complete. No book of its kind ever is, and the ones that claim to be are the least reliable on the shelf.

Where people disagree, and they generally do, I have let them speak for themselves. Where I disagree with them, I have tried to say so plainly. The reader should know that I am a scholar of the Lantern Conclave, and that the Conclave has opinions about almost everything. I have tried to keep mine in the margins. I have not always succeeded.

— Edra Moyle, reader in histories, Ambrel

### History

#### L1 · The Age of Gods
*Art: to pick*

Every temple in the world disagrees about the gods, except on how the world began. The gods walked among mortals, the story goes, raising the mountains and setting the rivers in their courses. When the work was finished they broke their own bodies to become its foundation, so that everything we stand on is, in some sense, a god. A few lesser gods chose to stay behind.

Scholars have no way to test the story, and most of us have stopped trying. It has left its mark on ordinary life all the same. In the lower wards of Ambrel, a labourer whose spade strikes bedrock will stop and say a word before he goes on, and a mason laying a foundation stone pours a cup of water over it first, the way you would offer a traveller a drink.

*"Mind where you dig. That's somebody."* — a stonecutter in Ambrel's lower wards

#### L2 · The Old Empire
*Art: to pick (a ruined imperial road or wall)*

The Old Empire built the roads we still travel, the walls we still shelter behind, and a good many of the arguments we still have. It was older than its own records. Its rulers took their orders from the gods directly, and with the gods behind it there was very little it could not do. By its own account it was a gentle master. Its own account is most of what survives.

It is hard now to understand how completely it leaned on the gods. Imperial harvests were sown on a priest's word, imperial cities were sited by vision, and imperial law, as far as we can tell, was whatever the gods said it was that year. When they fell silent, it had nothing else to stand on.

*"They had an answer for everything. They never wrote down the questions."* — Hollis Tarne, archivist of the Lantern Conclave

#### L3 · The Degeneration
*Art: to pick*

The Empire did not fall all at once. It declined for longer than anyone lived, so no one ever saw the whole of it, and what survives is mostly complaint: harvests that came in late, orders from the capital that contradicted the last ones, temple dues that rose while the temples argued among themselves. Historians call this the Degeneration. The people who lived through it mostly called it the way things were.

Factions grew up in those centuries around what the Empire ought to become, some wanting it purer, some freer and some gone. Every one of them is still with us. The arguments have hardly changed since. Only the people making them have.

#### L4 · The Cataclysm
*Art: to pick*

No event in history is better known or less understood. Every account written in the years after it reaches for the same picture: a rope that had been pulled taut for an age, suddenly running out through the hands. Something let go. The gods stopped speaking, and within a season the Empire was gone.

Nobody knows why, though there is no shortage of people willing to tell you. Every faith has its explanation, and every explanation has its villain, who is generally someone else.

*"It didn't break. It pulled away. Nobody wants to hear the difference."* — a summoner of the hill country, who would not give her name

#### L5 · The Silence
*Art: to pick*

The years after the Cataclysm were short and terrible. The Empire had lived on the gods' word, and without it the granaries emptied, the roads went quiet and the great cities began to drain away. Towns that had never needed walls learned very quickly why other towns had them.

It is called the Silence because that is what people remembered. They went on praying, as they always had, and for the first time in memory nothing answered. Much of what we know of those years comes from temple daybooks, in which the priests set down each morning's prayers and each morning's silence, sometimes for decades, until the books simply stop.

#### L6 · The present day
*Art: to pick (a living town or a stretch of road)*

A hundred and fifty years on, the world has largely recovered, and nobody is satisfied with the result. The new kingdoms hold, after a fashion. Each has a crack running through it that you could put your hand in, and each is quite sure the others are about to fall in theirs.

Most places keep to themselves. Walk two valleys over and the bread is different, the oaths are different, and someone will ask where you're from before they ask your name. Even the calendar will not agree. Ambrel counts its years from the founding of the university, the Vigil count them from the last year the gods spoke, and much of the countryside does not count them at all. Ambrel is the great exception to all of this, and we are insufferable about it.

*"Hard times. Not the end of the world. Not yet."* — an innkeeper on the Sallow road

### The gods

#### L7 · What people believe about the gods
*Art: Temple cleric*

Ask four people why the gods went quiet and you will get five answers. The common ones are these.

**They're dead.** *"We're only catching up."* — a barrow priest of the Gravebound

**They never existed.** *"A useful story. The Empire told it, and the Empire's gone."* — Ivo Saddler, lecturer of the Lantern Conclave

**They're disappointed in us.** *"We stopped listening first. They're waiting to see if we've learned."* — Mother Anwen of the Abbey of the Long Stair

**They're silent for our own good.** *"If they spoke now, we'd only ask them for things."* — a pilgrim on the shrine road

Some say the Velunn prove the gods were real. Others say the Velunn are only an old race with a high opinion of themselves. The Velunn, for their part, have declined to settle the matter.

#### L8 · The Velunn
*Art: Saint Maren of the Long Watch*

The Velunn are the lesser gods who stayed behind when the rest gave up their bodies, or so the temples teach. They are saints and devils rather than gods. Most people go their whole lives without meeting one, and most of those who have met one would call that a mercy. Those who dislike them call them the Veli, or the Vels, and do not trouble to lower their voices.

Each is known by name, and each name is a warning of a kind.
- **Saint Maren of the Long Watch,** who heals, and who sits at doors she thinks should stay shut.
- **Vashkar, Who Counts the Dead.** They say he's already counted you.
- **Ilvane, Who Asks Twice.** Answer carefully the first time.
- **Orrowen, Who Was Here First,** and never lets you forget it.

*"She looked at me like she'd known my grandmother. She probably had."* — a soldier of the Sunmane Oath

#### L9 · Summoners
*Art: to pick (a summoner with a visible mark)*

Since the Cataclysm, a few children in every generation have been born with a mark of gold on the skin, like an old scar that catches the light. In the south it is called the goldmark, and in the hill country the gilt scar. In some villages it is not called anything at all, and the child is simply watched. It runs in no particular family and favours no particular people.

Those who carry it can call, and things answer: things that ought not to be there, which come and fight at the summoner's side and are gone afterwards. Ask a summoner how it is done and they will tell you, quite honestly, that they have no idea.

*"It's like someone very far away is holding the other end."* — a summoner of the Lantern Conclave

### The alignments

#### L10 · The Vigil
*Art: Line of dawn*

The Vigil are the faithful of the old order, and they have kept faith for a hundred and fifty years without a word in reply. They want the Empire restored and the gods back in it. Their healers are the best in the world and their knights keep oaths everyone else has forgotten. Their children still learn their letters from imperial primers, so that a Vigil farm girl can recite the titles of emperors whose cities are now sheep pasture.

They are at home in old towns under old banners, and they are, almost without exception, kind. They have never quite forgiven the Conclave for the fall. We have never quite had the heart to tell them that nobody is coming.

*"Set another place. Just in case."* — a Vigil grandmother, at supper

#### L11 · The Gnosis
*Art: Lantern arcanist*

That is us, and I will try to be fair. The Gnosis hold that the world can be learned, as a language or a trade is learned, and that the Empire stopped learning long before it fell. We would rather build something new than dig up something old. We are a people of cities, Ambrel above all. We argue through the night, and we are usually right, eventually.

The others think us reckless. Some of us are. In the university's oldest hall the Conclave keeps a list of every scholar killed by their own work, and it is the custom for new students to read it aloud on their first day. It takes most of the morning.

*"Ask the question. Then ask who told you not to."* — Ilse Varro, examiner of the Lantern Conclave

#### L12 · The Dominion
*Art: Ashen Court noble*

I've been into the Dominion once. I won't go again.

Nobody there lives well except the people who own the others. The fields were tidy and the roads were safe, and nobody spoke to me the whole way through. When I asked a boy the name of his village, his mother pulled him inside. Through the door I heard her:

*"Quiet, now. They count the quiet ones last."*

#### L13 · The Fervor
*Art: Cinder berserker*

The Fervor found something worth keeping in the wreck of the Empire, and what they found was freedom. They mean to keep it. They live in bands and hosts rather than kingdoms, answer to no one they did not choose, and will pull down any throne they come across, including, on occasion, their own.

They are loud, brave, generous and impossible to govern, and the best company in the world until, quite suddenly, they are not. When a Fervor band takes a town, the first thing it burns is the tax roll. The second is usually the gallows.

*"Sit anywhere. This table hasn't got a head."* — a woman of the Cinder Clans

#### L14 · The Genesis
*Art: Elderwood stag*

The Genesis want the wild to grow back over the Empire, and over the rest of us if we are careless. They live in forest villages and in the trees themselves, in small bands that move on when the land seems to ask it. They hold that the Conclave would strip the hills for brass, that the Fervor would burn them, and that the Vigil are praying to an empty room. On a bad day I find two of the three hard to argue with.

They plant a tree over each of their dead, so a Genesis village is easiest to find by its grove, which is always older and larger than the village. Most of them are good neighbours. A few are the reason my colleagues no longer survey the forest roads.

*"Let the ground have him. It's owed."* — a Genesis elder, at a burial

### The factions

#### L15 · The Sunmane Oath
*Art: Oath knight*

If you have ever seen a lion folk knight in full armour, you have seen the Sunmane Oath at its best, and the Oath would very much like you to remember it. It fights to restore the Empire, and in the meantime it keeps every oath the Empire ever swore, on behalf of people a hundred and fifty years dead. Most of its spears, it should be said, are carried by human militia from the old border towns, who keep the oaths just as faithfully and get rather less of the credit.

Ask any of them about Sallow Bridge. Do not ask if you have somewhere to be.

#### L16 · The Still Flame
*Art: Temple cleric*

The Still Flame are the priests and healers of the Vigil. They have prayed for a hundred and fifty years to gods who do not answer, and they show no sign of stopping. Every shrine they keep has a lamp burning in it. They will heal anyone who is brought to them, friend or enemy, and ask after the state of your soul only once you are well enough to answer.

Their pilgrims walk the shrine roads from the spring thaw to the first frost, and most villages along the way will feed them, half out of kindness and half to be safe.

*"I have closed nine thousand wounds and heard no voice. I will close another tomorrow."* — a healer of the Still Flame

#### L17 · The Lantern Conclave
*Art: Cloistered scholar*

The Lantern Conclave is my own house, and the reader may discount what follows as they see fit. The Conclave are the scholars of magic, and their home is the university at Ambrel, the only great school of the Empire to survive its fall. Its examiners decide who may study there, which makes them exactly as popular as one would expect. Its surveyors go out after anything the Empire left half-measured, and as a rule they come back with more questions than they took.

Not all its students are scholars. Half the lamplighters in Ambrel learned their trade in its lower lecture halls. But its library holds more of the Empire than anywhere else in the world. A good deal of it has not been read since the Cataclysm, and some of it, the archivists suspect, has never been read at all.

#### L18 · The Artificers
*Art: Brass chorister*

The Artificers make things that work: lamps that light themselves, brass hands that lift what ours can't, engines nobody else can mend. Half of Ambrel runs on something they built, and the other half is waiting for a part. Their workshops are the loudest streets in Ambrel, and the warmest in winter.

#### L19 · The Ashen Court
*Art: Hostess of the late hour*

The Ashen Court is the Dominion's new order, and it keeps very good books. It collects its tithes in coin and souls. A debt to the Court passes on to you, then to your family, and in time to your body. Its envoys are perfectly courteous. That's the worst thing about them.

#### L20 · The Gravebound
*Art: Barrow priest*

The Gravebound tend the barrows and want the long fall finished. They do not hurry it. They are the most patient people in this book, and they seem to regard the rest of us as people who have not yet understood how the story ends.

In Gravebound country the barrows are kept better than the houses. Every child is taught the way to the family mound before they are taught the way home, and at the end the old are walked there, to spare their families the trouble of carrying them.

*"Lie down. It's easier."* — a barrow priest

#### L21 · The Warhorde
*Art: Orc warchief*

The Warhorde follows whoever is strongest this season. They're mostly orcs, goblins and giants, though I once met a human cook who had ridden with them for eleven years and outlived four chiefs. He said the trick was never to be the strongest.

*"Forty went up the pass with Grusha. Four hundred came down."*

#### L22 · The Cinder Clans
*Art: Cinderline veteran*

The Cinder Clans are the free hosts of the Fervor: bands of a few hundred, each led by a chief it chose at the fire and can unchoose at the next. A chief of the Clans holds office exactly as long as the band agrees to follow, and a wise one eats last.

They hire out their swords to anyone who pays and raid anyone who doesn't, and they keep careful count of which is which. Each band keeps a knotted cord of its debts, one colour for what it owes and another for what it is owed. A Cinder band will forgive a debt of coin. It has never been known to forgive one of blood.

#### L23 · The Thornwardens
*Art: Thornwarden pathguard*

Of all the Genesis, the Thornwardens are the ones the rest of us mean when we lower our voices. They keep no capital and crown no one. They are a fellowship of wardens, each sworn to a stretch of forest, and each defends it as a farmer defends a field: without the least interest in your reasons for being there.

The old north road out of Ambrel ran through the Hollin Wood for a thousand years, and it has been closed for three generations. Each time the city has cleared it, the Wardens have closed it again. The last company sent to reopen it was found the following spring, in good health, forty miles south, with no clear memory of the walk.

In the valleys along the forest's edge, woodcutters still leave the first cut of every felling at the treeline. Nobody will say who collects it.

#### L24 · The Elder Herd
*Art: Herd mother*

The Elder Herd is less a people than a following. Where the great beasts walk, the herd-folk walk with them, and they have done so since before the Silence. They camp where the herd beds down and strike camp when it rises, so a herd village may move forty times in a year without anyone in it feeling they have gone anywhere.

They do not name the great beasts, holding that a name is a kind of ownership, and they will not settle land the herd has passed without grazing. They have a saying that the herd is never lost; only the people who leave it are.

### The peoples

#### L25 · Humans
*Art: to pick (a varied crowd or a working scene)*

Humans are most of the world, and most of this book. I won't try to sum us up. I've met too many of us.

#### L26 · Lion folk
*Art: Pride matriarch*

The lion folk are an old and serious people, rarely seen outside their own high grasslands. They live in prides of several families under a matriarch, and her word binds the whole pride. A lion folk who breaks a promise their matriarch made has broken her word, not merely their own, and that is thought very much worse.

Small wonder, then, that the Sunmane Oath finds so many of its knights among them. An oath sworn a hundred and fifty years ago on behalf of the dead is no strange idea to a people who have always kept promises made by someone else.

Visitors to a pride are fed well, watched closely, and asked before they leave exactly what they mean to do next.

#### L27 · Goblins
*Art: Goblin cutter*

Goblins are small, quick, quarrelsome and very nearly impossible to discourage. They live wherever larger people have left a gap: in the broken hill country, along the forest margins, in the lee of other people's camps, and in a fair number of other people's cellars. Their households are large and loud, and they carry on their family quarrels for generations, with a care for detail that would shame a Conclave archivist.

Many ride with the Warhorde, where a small rider who never stops coming has the advantage of a large one who does. The goblins of the Copperlane will tell you they invented the firepot. Several other goblin families will tell you the Copperlane are liars.

#### L28 · Orcs
*Art: Orc raider*

Orcs are a hard people from hard country: broad, proud and slow to forgive. An orc takes the name of the last great fight they won, so a famous warrior may carry four or five names in a lifetime, and their oldest songs are largely lists of them.

They have a reputation, and they have earned it. An orc band in a rage will take a village apart and sing while it works. What is less often written down is that an orc camp will share its fire with any stranger who asks properly, and that an orc remembers a shared meal for the rest of their life, for good or ill.

#### L29 · Giants
*Art: Hill giant*

Giants are seldom seen and less understood. They keep to the high hills and mountains, in halls of fitted stone so large that travellers have mistaken them for ruins of the Empire, and a few of them are. They come down into the lowlands when the winters are hard, or when someone has given them reason to; the hill giants of the Warhorde have been given a great many.

They are long-lived and unhurried, and their stories begin far back. A Conclave scholar once spent a winter with a giant family in the Greyspine and came home with eleven notebooks of a single song. It had not yet reached the Cataclysm.

#### L30 · Alderkin
*Art: Alderkin waterkeeper (concept)*

The alderkin are the tree people of the old forests, as tall as a house and older than anyone can prove. They are not servants of the forest, nor of the Genesis, though the Genesis treat them with something near reverence. They have purposes of their own, and they have never felt the need to explain them.

Woodcutters in alderkin country knock on a trunk before they cut it, and walk away from any that knocks back. It sounds like a children's story until you have watched a grown woodcutter do it.

#### L31 · Riverfolk
*Art: Mending the hood*

Ambrel is a city of water, and the water is kept by the Riverfolk. Their divers clear the canals, mend the sluice gates and walk the floors of the old cisterns, and there is hardly a lock in the city they couldn't open blindfold, which in the murk below is how they open most of them. It is cold, hard work, and it has never paid as well as it should.

Outside the cities they live in small villages along the rivers and marshes, on stilts or in boats lashed together. At the first flood of spring every household sets a lamp on the water. A lowlander who comes to watch will be welcomed, fed and, almost certainly, pushed in.

#### L32 · Glimmerfolk
*Art: Glimmerfolk final reference*

Beneath the oldest districts of Ambrel lies the city ours was built upon, and the Glimmerfolk have lived there as long as the city above has stood. Their undercity is a quiet place of courts and long galleries, lit only for guests. Between the glimmers on their skin and the sharpness of their hearing, they have little use for lamps themselves.

They are a close and old-fashioned people. They live long, marry late and keep their courts as their grandparents kept them, and a young Glimmerfolk who moves up to the surface is spoken of in the tone we keep for those who run away to sea. Yet a lost surface-dweller who wanders down will be fed and walked home, and nothing more will be said of it.

Beyond the edges of the undercity, where it gives way to the sewers and to older diggings, even the Glimmerfolk go carefully.

#### L33 · Moor folk
*Art: Moor folk final lineup*

The high moors are poor country, wet and treeless, and the Moor folk who live there have never wanted any other. They keep steadings far apart, cut peat, raise a small and ill-tempered breed of sheep, and come together only at the four moots of the year, where marriages are made, debts settled and old quarrels either ended or carefully renewed.

They are a plain, wary people, and outsiders find them hard to know. The bog paths are their great secret. They mark them with nothing, and they guide strangers across for a price. Three lowland lords have tried to drain the moors in living memory. The moors are still there, and so, one assumes, are the lords.

#### L34 · The Ashborn
*Art: Ashborn final lineup*

The Dominion does not bury its dead. It drafts them. The Ashborn come back whole in body and thin in everything else, and the Ashen Court keeps them in its ledgers in the same columns as grain and iron.

I watched a column of them pass a Dominion border village. A woman at the roadside said, quite calmly, *"That one was my brother."* He didn't look at her. None of them did.

The Dominion's own people tell stories of Ashborn who rose to sit beside their masters, and of others whose touch leaves the living cold for days. They tell these stories quietly, and not to strangers.

#### L35 · The Banded folk
*Art: Banded folk working reference*

The salt roads belong to the Banded folk, and have since the Silence. They are one people in two colourings: the salt families of the white flats and the silt families of the river deltas, bound by trade, by marriage and by an inexhaustible supply of in-laws. Their caravans carry salt one way and grain the other across country that kills the careless, and the Banded folk are not careless.

They are hard and proud, quick to fight and quicker to judge whether a fight is worth it. They hold the Warhorde's rages in open contempt, which the Warhorde takes personally. A Banded caravan will not move in the heat of the day or on the first day of rain, and travellers who hire one soon learn to stop asking why.

#### L36 · Crest folk
*Art: Crest folk working reference*

Much of what the Empire built is still standing, and a good deal of it is still standing because of the Crest folk. They are the builders of the limestone valleys, where every town is a quarry town and every quarry belongs to a guild. A family's trade, its mason's mark and its debts are handed down together, as a single inheritance.

They are exacting people, and their great dispute is an old one: whether an imperial bridge should be mended exactly as it was built, or built better than it was. The question has split guilds and ended marriages. At Harrowmere, in the year of the long frost, it left a bridge half-mended in two different manners, and so it remains. Travellers say it is the safest crossing in the valley. Neither guild will admit it.

#### L37 · Greyhides
*Art: Greyhides working reference*

On the cold northern coasts, where the land breaks into islands and the sea is grey nine months of the year, live the Greyhides: a large, slow, gentle people who seem made for weather that would kill anyone else. Whole villages share their great longhouses, and any traveller who reaches one is fed and housed without question. To turn someone from the door is the worst shame they know, and a Greyhide who has done it is not soon forgiven.

They prize healers above every other craft, and their own are very good. Their kindness is easily taken advantage of, and often is. It is not, by all accounts, wise to do it twice.

#### L38 · The Saltreach
*Art: The Last Boat Up*

The Saltreach are a human people of the cold, rocky coasts: fishers and sailors in oiled wool, with shell and bone at the throat. Their friendship with the Greyhides is old and runs deep. A Saltreach boat in trouble will always make for the nearest Greyhide shore, and it is said that no Saltreach crew has ever been turned away from one.
