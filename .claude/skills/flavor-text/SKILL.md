# Breachline flavour text

Flavour text makes the world feel old, costly and real. The creator wants the setting taken seriously, because puns and jokes on a card break the spell of the painted art around it.

## Status: being worked out

Early lines failed because they were generic and had no grounding in the world. Read `breachline/SETTING.md` first and draw on the setting's own history, places and people. Details marked Proposed there aren't approved, so confirm them before leaning on them. Lines the creator has kept, rejected or not yet judged are in `breachline/FLAVOUR.md`, so check it before writing and add new lines there. Keep flavour text off cards until the creator asks for it. Never reveal the setting's secret truth about the gods outright; a line can only hint at it.

## Core voice

Take the world seriously, but vary the mood. Lines can be hopeful, proud, warm or dry as well as somber, and only puns and out-and-out jokes are ruled out. Let the line carry weight through what it implies.

- **Stop early.** End on the first line that lands. The creator cut second sentences that kept explaining, so test every line by deleting its last sentence.
- **Be about this card's subject.** Write about who this character is and what their life is like, instead of the setting's big themes. Don't describe their ability, which the rules text already covers.
- **Lean on the art.** Look at the card's image in `breachline/art/web/` before writing. A very short line can rely on what the picture shows, and the creator likes that approach.
- **Vary the emotion across a batch.** Mix cold, warm, funny, proud, hopeful and sad lines. The creator especially liked this variety.
- **Stay off the nose.** Don't have characters talk directly about the Empire or the gods' silence just to show the setting. Let the setting show up sideways, or not at all.
- **Use names sparingly.** A line can refer to a place or person that isn't defined anywhere, which makes the world feel larger, but the creator found a batch with a name on most lines too much. Aim for up to about 4 lines in every 20 to 30, and list each name in `breachline/FLAVOUR.md` so it can be normalized later.
- **Go shorter than feels safe.** The creator's favourites are often one short sentence or two very short ones. They also cut a line down to its first sentence more than once.
- **Vary the structure.** Specific numbers work well, but don't let every line depend on one.
- **Leave cards blank when nothing is good enough.** Not every card needs a line, and few lands do.

- Keep it to one or two sentences, and usually under 18 words, because the card has little room.
- Add something the art and rules don't already say. Restating the ability wastes the space.
- Tie the line to this card's subject and, where it helps, to something visible in its art.
- Use plain, timeless language with no modern idiom, so it sounds like it belongs to the world.
- A line can have no speaker, or be credited to a role, such as a Still Flame healer. A role grounds the line in the world without needing a named person.

## Techniques that work

These come from studying serious Magic flavour text. Hearthstone isn't a model, because its text is mostly jokes and fourth-wall humour. Write original lines using these structures, and never borrow another game's wording.

- **Proverb:** a short truth that works on its own, as though people in the world really say it.
- **A real person's words:** a letter, diary entry or last words, where one specific person makes a large event feel personal.
- **Faith under strain:** someone keeps up a duty or belief after the evidence has gone. This suits the Silence especially well.
- **A terrible fact stated plainly:** the line doesn't comment on it, and the reader feels the weight.
- **Character in a few words:** one brief statement shows a whole outlook, ideally the alignment's.
- **The unanswered mystery:** the line stops at what the speaker doesn't know.
- **Paired cards:** two cards answer each other, so rivals seem to argue across a collection.

Use specific details, such as a number, an object or a role, because they're what separate a line from generic gloom.

## What to avoid

These all make a card feel like it's winking at the reader:

- Puns and out-and-out jokes
- Irony, sarcasm or a punchline in the second half of the line
- Action-hero bravado
- Lines that would work equally well on any card

## Tone by colour

Match the line to the colour first, then keep it somber within that colour's energy.

| Colour | Alignment | Voice | Emotions to draw on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| White | Vigil | High-minded and solemn; speaks of duty and what is owed to others | Reverence, sacrifice, dignified grief, hope that costs something |
| Red | Fervor | High energy; short, driving and urgent, with hunger behind it | Fury, exhilaration, defiance, the emptiness after the fire |
| Blue | Gnosis | Esoteric and strange; speaks of knowledge as a dangerous thing | Curiosity, obsession, unease, awe at what shouldn't be known |

Black and green don't have a voice yet. Ask the creator before writing lines for them.

## Emotion by card type

Each card type adds its own feeling on top of the colour.

| Card type | Whose feeling it is | Emotions to draw on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unit | One person, or the people who know them | Resolve, fear, weariness, pride |
| Instant | Someone inside a single moment | Shock, desperation, sudden relief |
| Enchantment | Someone living with what lingers | Devotion, dread, slow corruption |
| Land | The place itself, which outlasts everyone | Awe, loneliness, memory |

Small units can sound frightened or weary, and the greatest ones can carry the burden of being followed.

## Before finishing

Read the line aloud and check that:

1. It would still feel right beside a serious oil painting.
2. It sounds like this colour and card type, and couldn't be moved to another card unchanged.
3. It has no pun or joke that asks for a laugh, and it doesn't go on after its best moment.

When offering options, give two or three lines that differ in emotion, not just wording.
